Stage 1 · Terms, Factors & Classification

Building Blocks of an Expression

Every algebraic expression is made of terms, joined by + or −. Each term is built from a coefficient and variable factors. Knowing the parts is the first step to combining them correctly.

Terms, Coefficients & Like Terms

Term = Coefficient × Variable(s)power e.g. in 7x²y, the coefficient is 7 and the factors are x², y
  • Like terms have the exact same variables raised to the exact same powers — only their coefficients may differ (e.g. 4xy and −9xy).
  • Unlike terms differ in variable or power (e.g. 4xy and 4x²y cannot be combined).
  • An expression is named by its number of terms: monomial (1), binomial (2), trinomial (3), or polynomial (4+).
Solved Exercise 1.1 — Identify the Parts Problem: List the terms and coefficients in 7x²y − 3xy + 5.
Terms: 7x²y, −3xy, 5
Coefficients: 7, −3, 5 (constant term)
Result: this is a trinomial — three unlike terms.
🦉 Owl's Lab Tip: "Check the variable letters AND their exponents — 3xy and 3x²y look like cousins, but they can never be added together!"

Stage 2 · Addition & Subtraction

Combining Expressions Safely

Adding or subtracting expressions is really just sorting tiles into matching piles — you can only merge terms that are alike, then add or subtract their coefficients.

The Combine Rule

(ax + by) ± (cx + dy) = (a ± c)x + (b ± d)y Group like terms first, then combine coefficients — variable part never changes
  • Rewrite subtraction as "add the opposite" — flip every sign inside the subtracted bracket.
  • Only coefficients change when combining; the variable and power stay exactly as they were.
  • An expression is fully simplified once no like terms remain to be combined.
Solved Exercise 2.1 — Subtract Two Expressions Problem: Simplify (5x + 3y) − (2x − 4y).
= 5x + 3y − 2x + 4y
= (5−2)x + (3+4)y
= 3x + 7y
Result: subtracting flips the sign of every term inside the second bracket first.
👧 Medhā's Observation: "Subtracting a bracket is like handing back tiles you borrowed — every tile inside changes to its opposite sign first!"

Stage 3 · Multiplication & Area Model

Multiplying Expressions: The Array Model

Multiplying two sums is the same as finding the area of a rectangle split into four smaller pieces — each piece is one pairwise product, and the total area is their sum.

The Distributive Law, Visually

(p + q)(r + s) = pr + ps + qr + qs Every term in the first bracket multiplies every term in the second — four products, four rectangles
  • Monomial × Binomial: distribute once, e.g. 3x(x + 4) = 3x² + 12x.
  • Binomial × Binomial: distribute twice — the area model keeps all four products organised.
  • The total area of the big rectangle always equals the sum of its four smaller regions.
Solved Exercise 3.1 — Expand a Binomial Product Problem: Expand (x + 3)(x + 5).
= x·x + x·5 + 3·x + 3·5
= x² + 5x + 3x + 15
= x² + 8x + 15
Result: middle terms 5x and 3x combine into 8x.
🦉 Owl's Wisdom: "Four rectangles, four products — lose track of one and your area model won't tile up perfectly!"

Stage 4 · Standard Identities

Unlocking the Three Master Identities

Some products appear so often that mathematicians memorised their expansions as identities — true for every value of a and b. Seeing them as squares makes them impossible to forget.

Geometric Proof by Squares

(a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b² A square of side (a+b) splits into an a² square, a b² square, and two ab rectangles
  • Identity I: (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b² — sum square.
  • Identity II: (a−b)² = a² − 2ab + b² — difference square.
  • Identity III: (a+b)(a−b) = a² − b² — difference of squares.
Solved Exercise 4.1 — Square a Number Instantly Problem: Find 105² using an identity.
105² = (100 + 5)²
= 100² + 2(100)(5) + 5²
= 10000 + 1000 + 25 = 11025
Result: no long multiplication needed!
👧 Medhā's Discovery: "Once you've seen the square split into four pieces, you'll never forget where that '2ab' comes from — it's the two matching rectangles!"

Stage 5 · Factorisation & Limiting Conditions

Undoing Multiplication: Factor Bottlenecks

Factorising x² + bx + c means finding two numbers that satisfy two conditions at once — their sum and their product. Just like a process can only run as fast as its weakest factor, a factorisation only "works" once both conditions are met together.

Two Conditions, One Answer

x² + (m+n)x + mn = (x + m)(x + n) Both m+n = b AND m·n = c must hold — matching only one condition isn't enough
  • List factor pairs of constant term c, then test which pair adds up to middle coefficient b.
  • Getting product right but sum wrong means factorisation is still incomplete.
  • Once both conditions match, the trinomial and brackets describe the exact same expression.
Solved Exercise 5.1 — Factorise by Testing Pairs Problem: Factorise x² + 7x + 12.
Factor pairs of 12: (1,12) (2,6) (3,4)
Check sums: 13, 8, 7 ✓
So x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)
Result: pair (3, 4) satisfies both product and sum together.
🦉 Owl's Diagnostic: "A guess that multiplies correctly but adds up wrong isn't a factorisation yet — you must fix the weaker condition first!"
